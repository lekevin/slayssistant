Rules Lawyer · retrieval over a board game rulebook

How the thing actually works

Every technique in this pipeline is here because something specific went wrong without it. This is the tour: what each method does, why it earned its place, what it costs — and how we know any of it helps, which is the part most RAG write-ups skip.

The constraint

One number drives the whole design

The entire corpus — rulebook plus a 382-entry card compendium — is about 23,000 tokens. Everything downstream follows from that.

At 472 chunks, the vector index is a 1.84 MB file of 32-bit floats. Scanning all of it with cosine similarity takes roughly a millisecond, which is about forty times faster than the network round trip to a hosted vector database would be before that database does any work at all. So the index is a file, committed to the repo, loaded once per cold start. There is no vector database because at this size one would make the system slower.

That's not a dodge around the hard part. Dense embeddings, BM25, rank fusion, structural chunking, page-accurate citations and a real evaluation harness are all still here. What's absent is the operational surface: migrations, connection pooling, index tuning, and a free tier that sleeps after a week of inactivity.

It does mean the honest baseline is brutal. At 23K tokens you can put the whole corpus in the prompt, cache it, and get perfect recall for about a penny a question. Any retrieval pipeline has to justify itself against that — not against a strawman. It's row 0 of the results table for exactly that reason.

Stage one · offline

Ingestion runs once and is then frozen

Ingestion is a separate, offline pipeline whose output is committed to the repo. It runs when the rulebook changes, not when a question arrives.

Why the parse output is frozen

Vision parsing is non-deterministic. Re-running it would produce slightly different text, which would invalidate every page reference and every label in the evaluation set. So it runs once, gets hand-checked, and is committed. A verification step gates any rebuild against that frozen copy.

Why chunk on headings rather than a fixed window

Rulebooks arrive pre-chunked by their authors — that's what numbered sections are. Splitting on the document's own structure means every chunk carries a truthful breadcrumb (Combat > Enemy Turn) and a real printed page range, which is what makes a citation resolvable. Undersized sections merge upward into their parent, never sideways into a sibling — a sibling's heading is the one that would show up in the citation, and it would be the wrong heading. Cards are never split: half a card is worse than no card.

Stage one · the sharp edge

Chunking mattered more than every retrieval trick combined

The keyword glossary originally parsed as one 573-token chunk holding fifteen unrelated definitions. Its embedding was therefore the average of fifteen meanings, which resembles none of them. Asked "what does Exhaust do?", retrieval returned seven cards that merely mention exhausting and never surfaced the definition at all.

Splitting definition lists one-per-term — the same rule that already made cards atomic — moved dense-only recall from 79.4% to 97.1%. No retrieval technique in the ablation moved anything close to that far. Chunking is the highest-leverage decision in the pipeline, and it is the one that gets the least attention in most write-ups.

That fix, though, has a sharp edge — and it is exactly the thing you asked about.

Found and fixed

Two real rules passages were missing from the index. Energy (Max 6) survived only by luck — the same limit is stated again on page 7, which was indexed. The Weak vs Vulnerable interaction is stated nowhere else in the corpus, so the assistant could not answer it at all.

The evaluation is what caught it. Question g090 missed on every arm, including the stuffed-prompt baseline that has the whole corpus in context. When a baseline with perfect coverage by construction still misses, the fault is upstream of retrieval — that's the tell, and it's why the baseline row is worth carrying.

The fix widened the pattern to accept blockquote callouts and longer terms, and made the branch keep whatever it doesn't claim instead of discarding it. Both passages are now atomic glossary chunks; g090 is kept in the set as a regression guard.

Stage two · per question

What happens when someone asks something

Why score-based gating fails

Ask "can I play a Trap during my opponent's turn?" of a rulebook that never discusses off-turn Traps. Retrieval returns the Traps section. A reranker scores it as highly relevant — correctly, it genuinely is about Traps. A score-based gate reads that as healthy evidence and withholds the search, handing the model a passage that is on-topic and silent. At that point the model can only guess.

The distinction a similarity score cannot express is relevance versus sufficiency. Nothing in a cosine value says "this passage is about your question and does not settle it." So prohibition-shaped questions instead get complete sections rather than fragments — absence is only assertable over a whole unit — plus web search attached unconditionally.

The classifier is a regex, deliberately tuned to over-trigger. A false positive costs one extra section of context and an unused tool. A false negative is a confidently fabricated permission that changes how someone plays their game. Those costs are not symmetric, so the threshold sits far toward recall. A router model would classify better, but it would add roughly 2.4 seconds to every question.

The methods

Four retrieval techniques, and what each one is for

These stack. Each one exists to cover a specific failure of the one before it, which is also exactly how the ablation tests them — one arm at a time, cumulative.

Dense embeddings

Arm 1 · meaning

Every chunk is converted into a list of 1,024 numbers positioning it in "meaning space," where texts about similar concepts land near each other. A question gets converted the same way, and we take whichever chunks sit closest — plain cosine similarity across all 472, no approximation, about a millisecond.

What it's good at: paraphrase. Someone asks "how do I get rid of a bad card" and it finds the section on Removing cards, despite sharing almost no words with it. Where it fails: rare exact tokens. Card names, keyword terms and numbers get smoothed into the general vicinity of their meaning, so "Gremlin Nob" can lose to a chunk that's merely about gremlins.

cost · one API call per question, ~250ms, cached by content hash

BM25

Arm 2a · exact words

Classic keyword scoring. A chunk scores well when it contains the question's words, with two adjustments that matter: rare words count for more than common ones (that's the IDF part — "Ethereal" is worth far more than "the"), and long chunks are penalized so they can't win just by containing everything.

What it's good at: precisely what dense retrieval fumbles — proper nouns, keyword terms, exact phrases. Where it fails: it cannot match a question to a passage that uses entirely different vocabulary, which is most naturally-phrased questions.

cost · free, runs locally in single-digit milliseconds

Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Arm 2b · combining the two

Dense and BM25 both produce ranked lists, but their scores are on incomparable scales — a cosine of 0.81 and a BM25 of 14.2 have no common unit. Any normalization you invent to blend them becomes a magic number you re-tune every time the corpus changes.

RRF sidesteps this by throwing the scores away and keeping only the positions. Each chunk gets 1/(60 + rank) from each list, summed. A chunk both methods like modestly beats a chunk one method loves — which is exactly the behavior you want from two retrievers with complementary blind spots.

cost · free · K=60 from the original paper, not tuned

Cross-encoder reranking

Arm 3 · reading properly

Everything above compares the question and the chunk separately — each was turned into numbers without ever seeing the other. A cross-encoder reads the question and one candidate passage together and scores how well that passage answers that question. It is substantially more accurate for the same reason it is slower: no shortcuts.

That cost is why it runs last, over roughly 20 surviving candidates rather than all 472. Its effect shows up most in ordering — in the results below it adds 5.9 points of recall but lifts MRR from 0.726 to 0.836, meaning the right passage moves toward the top of the list rather than merely being somewhere in it.

cost · one API call, ~16s across the full eval — the slowest stage by far

Fusion, concretely

Why ranks and not scores

Citations

The page number is computed, not reported

The feature that justifies the whole project is the page chip under each answer. It nearly didn't work.

The API returns citations with real start_page_number fields only for document blocks whose source is an actual PDF. This app sends assembled markdown, so every citation comes back as a char_location — character offsets, no page fields. Reading citation.page_location.start_page_number off one yields undefined, and the chip renders "Rulebook p.undefined" on the single feature that matters most.

We don't need the API to tell us the page. We assembled the string, so we know which chunk occupies which character range, and every chunk carries its printed page range from ingestion. The document index selects the span map, a binary search on the start offset finds the chunk, and the page comes from our own data. The API supplies the verbatim quoted text, which is the part it's genuinely authoritative about.

The evaluation

How we know whether any of it helps

This is the half that turns "I built a RAG chatbot" into a claim with evidence behind it.

Labels are text spans, never chunk IDs

The obvious design is to tag each question with "the chunk that should be retrieved." It cannot work. Chunk IDs are derived from content, so re-chunking regenerates them — and re-chunking is precisely what the ablation varies. Worse, the parse is a vision call, so even re-running an unchanged pipeline would break every label. A golden set that self-destructs on the experiment it exists to measure is not an evaluation, it's a trap.

Instead each question is labeled with the verbatim sentence that answers it. A retrieved chunk counts as a hit if it contains that sentence, however the chunker happened to draw its boundaries this time. That makes the set survive re-chunking, re-embedding and re-parsing. Matching is normalized and tolerates ~20% drift, so a slightly different transcription of the same page still matches.

The two retrieval metrics

Recall@8 — did the answer-bearing passage appear anywhere in the top 8 results? This is the one that matters most, because the model can read all 8. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) — how high did it appear? Rank 1 scores 1.0, rank 2 scores 0.5, rank 4 scores 0.25. Recall saturates quickly on a small corpus; MRR keeps measuring after it does, which is why reranking looks unimpressive on one and strong on the other.

Four statistics, because the effects are the size of the noise

A bare percentage on a hundred-odd questions is misleading, so the harness reports four things alongside it — each answering a question the percentage can't.

Wilson confidence interval

how firm is this number?

85.0% on 187 questions isn't 85.0% — it's "somewhere in 79–89%, probably." The interval says how much the number would wobble if you drew a different sample of questions. Publish the interval or the number means nothing.

McNemar's test

did arm B actually beat arm A?

Because both arms answer the same questions, the comparison must be paired. Only the disagreements carry information: questions B got and A missed, versus the reverse. An arm winning 8 and losing 1 and an arm winning 12 and losing 5 can post the identical headline and mean opposite things.

Holm correction

four comparisons, not one

Run four tests at the usual 5% threshold and you have roughly a 1-in-5 chance of a false positive somewhere. Holm tightens the bar to compensate. It's the difference between "this ladder shows a real improvement" and "one of my four coin flips came up heads."

Minimum detectable effect

what could this set even prove?

The most useful number here, and the reason you're expanding the question set. At n=187 the MDE is 10.2 points: any gap smaller than that is indistinguishable from luck, no matter what the table says. It states the experiment's resolution up front instead of letting a reader over-read a small difference.

The metric that matters

Recall cannot see the failure this system was built to avoid

Everything above measures whether the right passage was found. None of it measures what the system then told the player — and that gap is where the whole risk lives.

A pipeline can post 98% recall and still be untrustworthy. Retrieve the Traps section, then invent a permission the section does not contain, and the retrieval scorer records a hit. So a second harness (npm run eval:answers) runs the real answering path over all 119 questions and has a separate judge classify each answer's stance — never showing it the expected label.

The production prompt, retrieval options, effort settings and search gating all live in lib/answer.ts and are imported by both the route and the eval. A second copy would drift within a week, and the metric would quietly start describing a system nobody ships.

False-permission rate

5.6% — 4 of 71 questions where the rulebook does not grant permission were answered as though it does. 95% CI 2–14%.

Why one number is never enough

A system that answered "the rules don't say" to everything would score a perfect 0% and be worthless. The guard is the permitted stratum, reported beside it: 43 of 48 correct, with 5 over-refusals. Two of those five are the authority ladder working as designed — asked what a specific card does, the model declined to state it flatly, because that text comes from the fan-transcribed compendium the prompt ranks below the rulebook.

The headline is an upper bound

A flag means the judge saw a "yes." It does not yet mean the system fabricated one, and reading the four is what separates the two. Only one is a clear fabrication: asked whether a card-reward pick can be swapped after the fact — genuinely unsettled by the rules — the model answered yes and cited the Full Knowledge rule, which governs looking before you finalize and says nothing about undoing a finalized choice. That is exactly the failure the metric exists to catch.

Of the remaining three, one is a badly-formed question of mine (asking whether the Watcher can sit in Wrath "without any downside" — the answer says yes-you-can-stay, then names the downside), and two look like label bugs rather than model failures. On the Scry question the model's reading of "you can't Scry more cards than are in your draw pile" as scry fewer is arguably better than the label it was graded against. The metric's job is to surface the rows worth reading; the triage stays manual, and saying so is part of reporting it honestly.

Results · 31 Aug 2026

What the harder question set revealed

The set grew from 34 to 187 scorable questions in three passes, deliberately moving away from the rulebook's own FAQ wording toward how people actually ask. The effect on the table was dramatic — and it's the clearest possible argument for why sample size and question quality matter more than any tuning.

Three things this table now says that the old one couldn't

The arms separated. On the FAQ-derived set, four of five arms scored between 95% and 100% — the test wasn't hard enough to distinguish them, so it flattered everything. The full stack now leads the keyword baseline by 11.3 points rather than the 0.3 it appeared to lead by before. Nothing about the pipeline changed; the measurement got honest.

The naive baseline stopped looking competitive. The old set was seeded largely from the rulebook's own FAQ, whose distinctive vocabulary is exactly what keyword search is best at — a selection bias that flattered the simplest method. Phrase questions the way players actually phrase them and keyword scoring drops over 12 points, while the reranked stack loses 3.7.

Reranking earns its latency in ordering, not coverage. It adds 5.9 points of recall but lifts MRR from 0.726 to 0.836 — the answer moves to the top of the list rather than merely being somewhere in the top 8. The chunker fix showed this precisely: the recovered passage sat in the fused candidate pool but outside the top 8, and only the reranker pulled it up into view. Hybrid alone did not move at all.

One rung resolved, two not

At n=187 the resolution is 10.2 points. The rerank step is the one arm that clears its own noise floor: it wins 15 and loses 4 against hybrid retrieval, McNemar p = 0.019, Holm-adjusted 0.058 — a hair outside the conventional threshold, which is the correct amount of hedging for four comparisons on one dataset, not a reason to round it down to nothing. The two middle rungs — dense over keyword, and BM25 fusion over dense — remain unresolved. 5-point resolution would need about 470 questions.

Open

What's genuinely unfinished

The freeze guard checks a recorded hash, not the file. docSha is written into rulebook.meta.json at parse time and copied forward into the index manifest, but it is never recomputed from the parsed markdown. The documented workflow is to hand-fix headings after parsing, and those edits change the bytes without changing the recorded hash — the current file hashes to 0e6bad66 while everything downstream still says 50bfb512. Nothing is broken today, because the golden set and the manifest share the same label, but the guard cannot currently detect the drift it exists to catch.

The false-permission set is too small to catch a regression. The metric now exists, but 71 questions puts its confidence interval at 2–14% — a real regression would have to be large before the number moved convincingly. The same sample-size problem as the retrieval table, on the measurement that matters most.

Two golden rows need re-labeling. Triaging the flagged answers turned up labels that are arguably wrong rather than answers that are — the Scry row in particular. A metric that surfaces bugs in its own answer key is working; leaving them in place is not.

The set should keep growing. 187 → 470 is the next meaningful threshold, the point at which 5-point gaps become resolvable. The highest-value additions are more unstated questions, since those are both the hardest to retrieve for and the ones where a confident wrong answer does real damage.
